## 4. Physical models

### 4.1 Trap potential, ion crystal, motional modes, micromotion and heating

#### 4.1.1 Potential and Mathieu equation

Near the trap centre the potential is quadrupolar in both its static and its rf part **[verified]** (Leibfried, Blatt, Monroe, Wineland, RMP 75, 281 (2003), Eqs. 1-2):

Φ(x, y, z, t) = ½ U (α x² + β y² + γ z²) + Ũ cos(ω_rf t) · ½ (α′ x² + β′ y² + γ′ z²),

with Laplace's equation forcing α + β + γ = 0 and α′ + β′ + γ′ = 0, which is why no static minimum exists. Each Cartesian coordinate obeys a Mathieu equation. The simulator adopts the RMP 2003 convention **[verified]**:

d²x/dξ² + [a_x − 2 q_x cos(2ξ)] x = 0,  ξ = ω_rf t / 2,  a_x = 4 Z|e| U α / (m ω_rf²),  q_x = 2 Z|e| Ũ α′ / (m ω_rf²),

i.e. a factor 4 in the static parameter and 2 in the rf parameter, with q_y = −q_x for a linear trap. Two convention facts the code must encode explicitly: (i) Wineland et al. 1998 and Berkeland et al. 1998 write the driven term with the opposite sign, +2 q cos, and Landa et al. 2012 with the minus sign; the sign of q is a half-rf-period time shift, so β and all frequencies are unchanged, but the phase of micromotion relative to the rf drive flips **[verified]**; (ii) β is even in q, so q_y = −q_x does not split the two radial secular frequencies; they differ only if a_x ≠ a_y **[corrected]** (a verifier caught the opposite inference in an extracted item; it is not in the source). Brownnutt et al. 2015 use the same factors (a_x = 4|e|Φ″_dc/(m Ω²), q_x = 2|e|Φ″_rf/(m Ω²)) **[verified]**, and Berkeland et al. give the linear-trap geometry explicitly, a_x = a_y = −a_z/2 = −4 Q κ U₀/(m Z₀² Ω²), q_x = −q_y = 2 Q V₀/(m R′² Ω²), q_z = 0 **[verified]**, where R′ is the effective radial scale with R′ ≅ R, equal to R for infinitely long hyperbolic electrodes; the extraction's R′ ≥ R was wrong, and the paper's own example uses R′ ≅ R = 1.0 mm with q_x ≈ 2√2 ω_x/Ω = 0.28 **[corrected]**. No source gives the geometry factors κ, R′, Z₀ or Φ″ for any electrode design, and none treats surface-electrode geometry at all; for such traps the simulator takes Φ″_dc and Φ″_rf from a user-supplied field solution (FEM or the gapless-plane approximation) through the geometry-free map above and locates the rf null from the Dehmelt pseudopotential Ψ = q²|E_rf(r)|²/(4mΩ²) **[background]**.

**Characteristic exponent and stability.** To lowest order β_x ≈ (a_x + q_x²/2)^{1/2} and the secular frequency is ν = β_x ω_rf/2 **[verified]**; the preprint form β = [(a + q²/2)/(1 − 3q²/8)]^{1/2}, dropped from the published Wineland 1998, is far more accurate, 0.12% from the exact exponent at q = 0.3 against 1.8% for the bare lowest-order form **[corrected]**. Stability in three dimensions requires 0 ≤ β_i ≤ 1 for every axis, and the Floquet root search must be restricted to that branch because the determinant also vanishes on the lattice ±β_j + 2n **[verified]**; traps run in the lowest stability region with a ≈ 0 and q < 0.5 **[background]**. For the exact β the printed continued fractions of RMP 2003, Eqs. (10)-(11), carry an off-by-one index error and cannot be used as an algorithm **[corrected]**; a verifier derived the correct recursion from Eq. (9), D_{2n} = [a_x − (2n + β_x)²]/q_x. The simulator will instead compute β by the monodromy method **[background]**: integrate the Mathieu equation over one period in ξ (π) for the two fundamental solutions, form the 2×2 monodromy matrix M, declare stability when |tr M| < 2, and obtain cos(πβ) = tr M / 2. This is convention-independent and is tested against the lowest-order formula at small (a, q) and against the stability boundary q ≈ 0.908 at a = 0.

**Pseudopotential.** Averaging over the rf period gives a harmonic pseudopotential; with a ≈ 0 the radial secular frequency is ω_r = Q V₀/(√2 Ω_T m R²) with Q the ion charge (Wineland 1998 Eq. 6, whose symbol q is the charge) **[verified]**, equivalently ω_r = q Ω_T/(2√2) with q the Mathieu parameter; the two symbols collide throughout the sources and are separated in code as Q and q (Section 13) **[corrected]**. This formula is bound to the q definition it comes with **[verified]**; the module therefore exposes secular frequencies computed from (a, q) rather than a stand-alone voltage formula, and provides voltage-to-(a, q) converters for the documented geometries.

**Trajectory and intrinsic micromotion.** To first order in a and second order in q (Wineland 1998, Eq. 4) **[verified]**:

u_i(t) = A_i { cos(ω_i t + φ_i) [1 + (q_i/2) cos(Ω_T t) + (q_i²/32) cos(2Ω_T t)] + β_i (q_i/2) sin(ω_i t + φ_i) sin(Ω_T t) }.

The secular motion is multiplied, not added to, by a micromotion modulation of fractional amplitude q_i/2 at Ω_T **[verified]** (Wineland's rf phase origin, a_i + 2q_i cos Ω_T t; under the plan's adopted Mathieu sign, Section 13, every Ω_T t in that trajectory reads Ω_T t + π, the only change); axial motion in a linear trap (q_z = 0) has no intrinsic micromotion **[verified]** (Berkeland's Eqs. 7-13 were checked against page renders because that PDF's text layer mangles its mathematics font). In the quantum treatment (RMP 2003, Eqs. 16-29, 37) **[verified]** the motional Hamiltonian H = p²/2m + (m/2) W(t) x² with W(t) = (ω_rf²/4)[a_x + 2 q_x cos(ω_rf t)] in the RMP's rf phase origin (the plan's adopted Mathieu sign has −2q_x cos(ω_rf t); see below) is solved by a c-number Floquet function u(t) with u(0) = 1, u̇(0) = iν, whose Wronskian is fixed at 2iν; the ladder operators of a static reference oscillator of frequency ν are constants of motion, and x̂(t) = x₀[â u*(t) + â† u(t)]. To lowest order, in that rf phase origin, u(t) ≈ e^{iνt}[1 + (q_x/2) cos(ω_rf t)]/(1 + q_x/2); in the sign the plan adopts for the Mathieu equation (Section 13: d²x/dξ² + [a − 2q cos 2ξ]x = 0, so W(t) = (ω_rf²/4)[a_x − 2q_x cos(ω_rf t)]) the same solution reads u(t) ≈ e^{iνt}[1 − (q_x/2) cos(ω_rf t)]/(1 − q_x/2), the two differing by half an rf period in the time origin and in nothing else, and the simulator's rf phase reference is the adopted sign: the Floquet recursion gives c_{±1}/c₀ = −q_x/((2 ± β)² − a) under it (`check_critique_v3.py` integrates the Mathieu equation under both signs), so the in-phase micromotion at the rf phase origin is a contraction, x_μ(t) = −(q_x/2) x_sec(t) cos(ω_rf t), and the phase of every micromotion sideband and the sign with which a dc shim adds to Berkeland's irreducible φ_ac term (below) follow from that one statement; the regression is that the modulation index of Section 4.3.6 changes sign, a phase step of π in the rf-photon correlation signal, as the shim voltage crosses the compensated value (the 2026-09-04 experimentalist critique found this paragraph in the RMP's sign against Section 13's, which left every micromotion phase undetermined) **[corrected: critique, 2026-09-04]**, **[recomputed here]**. The first two revisions of this plan read the normalization (1 + q_x/2)^{−1} of that form as a factor C₀ rescaling the effective Lamb-Dicke parameter, η_eff = ηC₀, following the RMP; the derivation audit of 2026-09-04 (Appendix D) showed that this is an artifact of normalizing u to u(0) = 1 at one rf phase. With x₀ = √(ħ/2mν) and [â, â†] = 1 the canonical commutator [x̂, p̂] = iħ fixes the normalization of the exact Floquet function through its Wronskian, Im(u* u̇) = ν, not through u(0) = 1 (the exact u cannot satisfy both: scaled to u(0) = 1 it has u̇(0) = iν(1 + q) in the adopted sign and iν(1 − q) in the RMP's rf phase origin, to first order in q; `check_critique_v3.py`), and under that normalization the e^{iνt} Fourier component of u, which is what the secular sideband couples to, is C₀ = [Σ_n c_n²(1 + nω_rf/ν)]^{−1/2} = 1 + 3q_x²/16 + O(q⁴), slightly greater than one, even in q and with no first-order term: 1.001890, 1.007741 and 1.018161 at q = 0.1, 0.2 and 0.3 against 0.952, 0.909 and 0.870 for (1 + q/2)⁻¹ (`check_c0_floquet.py`; the u(0) = 1 average depends on the rf phase assigned to t = 0 and runs from 0.86 to 1.18 at q = 0.3 as that phase moves by half an rf period, while a measured sideband Rabi frequency cannot depend on the rf clock) **[corrected: derivation audit, 2026-09-04]**, **[recomputed here]**. The simulator's C₀ is therefore the Wronskian-normalized rf-period Fourier coefficient from the monodromy module, with 1 + 3q²/16 as its O(q²) check, and it multiplies η in exactly one place, `Crystal.lamb_dicke` (Appendix E), which records it in the provenance of every η; the drive builder of Section 4.3.6 and the Hamiltonian of Section 5.7 receive η with C₀ already inside and never reapply it, and a test compares η at q = 0.3 with η at q = 0 against 1 + 3q²/16 to O(q⁴) (the 2026-09-04 architect critique found three sections naming the multiplication and none naming its owner, so that η could have carried C₀² or C₀⁰). Its size is not negligible: C₀ shifts η by 1.8% at q = 0.3, first order in every sideband Rabi frequency and 3.7% in the Mølmer-Sørensen two-body phase (∝ η²Ω²), an uncalibrated infidelity near 10⁻³; what makes it harmless is that the sideband amplitude calibration of Section 7.5 absorbs it, so the defensible statement is that C₀ is absorbed by calibration, not that it sits at the η⁴ level as the second revision said, and the case in which it matters is a calibration made on the carrier rather than on the sideband, for which Section 9.17 pins the pseudopotential-versus-Floquet η difference as a validation case instead of dismissing it (2026-09-04 experimentalist critique) **[corrected: critique, 2026-09-04]**; the 5 to 13% reductions of η that the earlier revisions carried were spurious. In the interaction picture the laser couples to the motion only through u(t), so sidebands appear at detunings δ = l′ ω_rf + l ν with the selection rule l ≠ 0 if l′ ≠ 0 **[corrected]** (the extracted item had inverted it): an ion at the rf null has no first-order micromotion sideband of the carrier, because intrinsic micromotion is proportional to the secular displacement. Note that RMP 2003 writes ν for an angular frequency throughout **[corrected]**.

**Excess micromotion** (Berkeland et al. 1998) **[verified]**. A uniform stray field E_dc displaces the ion to u_{0i} = Q E_dc·û_i/(m ω_i²) and produces excess micromotion of amplitude (1/2) u_{0i} q_i; an rf phase difference φ_ac between opposite electrodes adds an irreducible term of amplitude (1/4) q_x R α φ_ac along x that no dc shim can null. In the ion frame the laser phase is modulated, k·u′(t) = β cos(Ω t + δ) (the printed Eq. 21 contains a stray factor Ω, confirmed as a misprint **[corrected]**), and the drive splits into a comb of components weighted by J_n(β): the carrier is suppressed by J₀²(β) and the first micromotion sideband has strength ratio J₁²(β)/J₀²(β) ≈ (β/2)² for β ≪ 1 **[verified]**. The simulator implements excess micromotion as an optional phase modulation of the drive coefficient, e^{iβ_mm cos(Ω_rf t + δ)}, with β_mm computed from the configured stray field, and reports the implied second-order Doppler shift **[verified]** for completeness.

**Explicit rf dynamics (optional module).** Landa et al. 2012 show that expanding about the pseudopotential minima does not diagonalize the rf problem: the modes obey a coupled Mathieu system with a π-periodic inhomogeneous drive (their Eqs. 11-13) **[verified]**, and to leading order every ion's micromotion amplitude is locked to its mean position, B₂ = −(q_α/4) B₀, i.e. peak excursion (q/2) times the ion's displacement from the rf null **[verified]**. Their Floquet-Lyapunov construction for the classical time-dependent modes is available **[verified]**, with two cautions the verifier established: Γ(t) is not unitary for a generic coupled system, and the symplectic normalization V^t(0)U(0) = (i/2)·1 is a matrix identity. Recovering the pseudopotential modes needs two conditions the paper states separately: that the mean ion positions of the periodic solution tend to the pseudopotential minima, which the paper says needs further investigation, and that the rf potential is isotropic with respect to the crystal so that Q is proportional to the identity; the micromotion-modulated Coulomb coupling K₂ is one reason the true modes differ, not the only one **[corrected]**. The quantized multi-ion Floquet frame is absent from the source set (Landa defers it to a companion paper), so every N > 1 result here is in the pseudopotential frame; the simulator's default is the pseudopotential-plus-C₀ treatment, with Landa's construction as a validation option because it multiplies the time-step budget by Ω_rf/ω_m.

#### 4.1.2 Equilibrium positions of the chain

For N identical ions in a harmonic axial well with transverse confinement strong enough to keep them collinear (James, Appl. Phys. B 66, 181 (1998), Eqs. 2.1-2.9) **[verified]**:

V = Σ_m ½ M ν² x_m² + Σ_{m≠n} (Z² e²/8πε₀) / |x_n − x_m|,

with equilibrium positions from ∂V/∂x_m = 0. With the length scale ℓ = (Z² e²/(4πε₀ M ν²))^{1/3} and u_m = x_m/ℓ the equilibrium conditions are N coupled algebraic equations,

u_m − Σ_{n<m} 1/(u_m − u_n)² + Σ_{n>m} 1/(u_m − u_n)² = 0,

with closed forms for N = 2 (u = ∓(1/2)^{2/3}) and N = 3 (u = ∓(5/4)^{1/3}, 0). James's Table 1 gives the numerical positions for N = 2 to 10; all 54 values were re-solved by a verifier with maximum relative deviation 1.9 × 10⁻⁵, so tests compare with rtol 10⁻⁴ **[verified]**. Wineland 1998 gives the same scale as s = (q²/(4πε₀ m ω_z²))^{1/3} with s₂ = 2^{1/3} s and s₃ = (5/4)^{1/3} s **[verified]**. The zigzag threshold is Section 1's verified result: ω_r/ω_z > 1 (N = 2), > 1.55 (N = 3, exactly √(12/5)), and (ω_r/ω_z)_c ≈ 0.73 N^{0.86} at large N, with the competing fits 0.63 N^{0.865} and 0.59 N^{0.885} listed in the same source **[verified]**. Marquet's α_crit = 2/(μ_N − 1) is the exact finite-chain condition and replaces the fits: (ω_r/ω_z)_crit = 1.0000, 1.5492, 2.4975, 4.5957, 6.5396, 7.2931 and 11.9930 for N = 2, 3, 5, 10, 15, 17 and 30, against 1.325, 1.878, 2.914, 5.288, 7.495, 8.347 and 13.603 from 0.73N^{0.86}, which is 13 to 15% conservative for N ≥ 10 and 32% high at N = 2 **[corrected]**; the force-balance form ω_r² = (7/(8πε₀)) ζ(3) q²/(m s_c³) reproduces the paper's 9Be+ example at 7.806 MHz **[verified]**. The crystal module solves the equilibrium equations by Newton iteration from the James table as initial guess, checks the zigzag criterion, and refuses to build a linear-chain mode structure when it is violated.

#### 4.1.3 Normal modes

**Axial.** The dimensionless Hessian (James Eq. 3.3; Marquet, Schmidt-Kaler, James 2003 Eq. 2.9) **[verified]**:

A_mn = 1 + 2 Σ_{p≠m} 1/|u_m − u_p|³ for m = n,  A_mn = −2/|u_m − u_n|³ for m ≠ n.

Its eigenvectors b^{(p)} are orthonormal and complete, its eigenvalues μ_p are numbered in increasing order, and the mode frequencies are ν_p = √μ_p ν. For every N, μ₁ = 1 with b^{(1)} = (1, …, 1)/√N (centre of mass) and μ₂ = 3 with b^{(2)} ∝ u (breathing or stretch); μ₃ = 5.8 exactly for N = 3 (29/5) and 5.81, 5.818, 5.824, 5.829, 5.834, 5.838, 5.841 for N = 4 to 10 **[verified]**. James's Table 2 eigenvectors are sign-consistent with the closed forms for all N **[corrected]** (an extracted item claimed otherwise; the verifiers checked every row). The simulator fixes the eigenvector sign by making the last component positive, the convention Marquet et al. use **[verified]**, and tests reproduce the tabulated spectrum with rtol 10⁻³ (the tables carry four significant figures).

**Transverse.** With anisotropy α = (ω₃/ω₁)² (axial over transverse, squared) the transverse Hessian is B_mn = (1/α + 1/2) δ_mn − (1/2) A_mn, which shares the eigenvectors of A and has eigenvalues γ_p = 1/α + 1/2 − μ_p/2 **[verified]**. A linear Paul trap has two transverse families, not one: with the two radial secular frequencies ω_x and ω_y along the principal axes (rotated about the trap axis by `Trap.axis_angle_rad`, Appendix E) the anisotropies are α_x = (ω₃/ω_x)² and α_y = (ω₃/ω_y)², the Hessians B^{(x)} and B^{(y)} carry 1/α_x and 1/α_y in the diagonal term and share A's eigenvectors, so γ_p^{(x)} = 1/α_x + 1/2 − μ_p/2 and γ_p^{(y)} = 1/α_y + 1/2 − μ_p/2 give two generally non-degenerate radial families of N modes each, identical eigenvectors and different frequencies Ω_p^{(x)} = ω₃√γ_p^{(x)} and Ω_p^{(y)} = ω₃√γ_p^{(y)}, which is why Section 4.4.7's reconciliation needs 2N radial modes, why `Crystal` declares 3N modes, and what the crystal module's unit test asserts (3N modes in three families; the 2026-09-04 experimentalist critique found only the single-α form written down, from which an implementer builds half the radial spectrum and leaves the other family's phase-space loops open) **[corrected: critique, 2026-09-04]**. Within each family the transverse frequencies are Ω_p = ω₃ √γ_p, so their ordering is reversed: the centre-of-mass mode is the highest transverse mode and the zigzag mode (largest μ) the lowest, and the chain buckles when γ_N → 0, i.e. at α_crit = 2/(μ_N − 1) **[verified]**. This matches the extracted ordering statement from Monroe's notes and the measured 32-ion 171Yb+ transverse COM modes at 4.19 and 4.05 MHz **[extracted]**.

**Quantization and per-ion Lamb-Dicke parameters.** The simulator quantizes each mode as x̂_k = x_{0,k}(a_k + a_k†) with x_{0,k} = (ħ/(2 m ω_k))^{1/2} (the standard convention; James uses the Kittel convention with an explicit factor i and (a − a†), which the code must not mix in **[verified]**). The displacement of ion j resolves into modes through the eigenvector components, and the Lamb-Dicke parameter of ion j for mode k under a drive with effective wavevector Δk is

η_{j,k} = b_j^{(k)} (Δk · ê_k) (ħ/(2 m ω_k))^{1/2},

which is Wineland 1998 Eq. 33 with the normal-mode matrix D projected onto the laser wavevector **[verified]** and James Eqs. 3.18-3.19 with his coupling constants s_m^{(p)} = √N b_m^{(p)} μ_p^{−1/4} (so that s = 1 for the COM mode) **[verified]**. The mode dependence of η therefore enters through both the eigenvector component and the mode frequency.

#### 4.1.4 Anharmonic corrections (optional module)

Expanding the Coulomb-plus-trap potential to third order gives a cubic term −(M ω₃²/(2ℓ)) Σ C_mnp ξ_{m3}(2 ξ_{n3} ξ_{p3} − 3 ξ_{n⊥} ξ_{p⊥}) with a fully symmetric dimensionless tensor C_mnp; in mode space the coefficients D_pqr obey two exact selection rules: Σ_p C_mnp = 0 makes the centre-of-mass mode decouple from all three-mode mixing (D_mn1 = 0 for every N), and Σ_p u_p C_mnp = ½(δ_mn − A_mn) fixes the stretch-mode coefficients D_mn2 = ((1 − μ_m)/(2𝒩)) δ_mn **[verified]** (Marquet et al. 2003, Eqs. 2.11, 2.20, 3.1-3.5; the verifier reproduced the paper's Appendix B tables numerically). The quantized interaction is −3 ε ħ ω₃ Σ D_mnp (γ_m γ_n μ_p)^{−1/4} [2 a_p (b_m† b_n + c_m† c_n) e^{iΔ^{(−)} ω_z t} + a_p (b_m† b_n† + …) e^{iΔ^{(+)} ω_z t} + h.c.] after dropping never-resonant terms, with a small dimensionless nonlinearity ε (1.06 × 10⁻³ for 9Be+ at ω₃ = 2π × 5.0 MHz) **[verified]**. The coupling strength is g = εω₃: ε = 7.09 × 10⁻⁴ for 40Ca+ at 2 MHz and 3.79 × 10⁻⁴ for 171Yb+ at 0.2 MHz axial give g/2π = 1419 Hz and 76 Hz. The first version of this plan multiplied g by the gate time and reported phases of 0.12 to 0.89 rad over published gates; that was arithmetic applied to the wrong physics, caught by the 2026-09-04 experimentalist critique: the cubic term is odd in the mode coordinates, its diagonal Fock elements vanish, and away from a three-mode resonance its leading effect is second order, a dispersive shift of order g²/Δ_res with virtual population (g/Δ_res)², Δ_res = |ω_m ± ω_n − ω_p| the distance to the nearest resonance. Direct integration of two modes coupled by g(a + a†)²(b + b†) at g/2π = 1.419 kHz (`check_anharmonic.py`) gives, over 100 µs, 0.003 to 0.004 rad on |1, 0⟩ for mismatches from 1 MHz down to 30 kHz and 0.016 rad on the resonant pair |2, 0⟩ ↔ |0, 1⟩ at a 100 kHz mismatch, against the 0.89 rad first-order figure; a coherent 0.89 rad phase would cost an infidelity of 0.19, two hundred times the total error of the Ballance gate this plan anchors, and that measured bound is the sanity constraint every estimate of this channel must satisfy **[corrected]**, **[recomputed here]**. The exact sum rule D_mn1 = 0 protects only the centre-of-mass mode of each family, which transverse gates do not use; the simulator ships the resonance checker with the second-order estimate g²/Δ_res per mode triple on by default, reports linear-in-time growth only when Marquet's resonance condition (Eq. 4.10) is met within the coupling width, and offers the cubic Hamiltonian itself as an opt-in term, because including it couples all three mode families into one joint space, which the mode-reduction strategy of Section 5 cannot afford by default; resonances occur only when the anisotropy satisfies Eq. 4.10 of that paper, a necessary condition that must be confirmed by substitution **[verified]** (the verifier reproduced the paper's N = 6 example from scratch, μ₅ = 13.513882, μ₆ = 18.265709, D₆₅₅ = 4.2527958, α_res = 0.09150975; the paper's 7.3556 comes from the rounded μ₅ = 13.51, the exact value being 7.35510). Wineland 1998 Eqs. 76-78 give the complementary static-field anharmonicity: first-order terms renormalize mode frequencies, second-order terms couple three modes resonantly when |ω_l ± ω_m| = ω_k with linear-in-time growth **[verified]** (the printed Eq. 78 reads D_k^i q_i q_m **[corrected]**). The module ships the resonance checker and warns when a configured trap sits near a three-mode resonance; the cubic Hamiltonian itself is an opt-in term, because with ε ~ 10⁻³ it matters only for gates near resonance or long idle times.

#### 4.1.5 Motional heating and motional decoherence

**Master equation.** A mode coupled to a thermal environment obeys (Wineland 1998 Eq. 62 with the journal's printed typo 2aρ†a† corrected to 2aρa†, the form both arXiv versions of the paper print, so the correction is the authors' own text (errata check of 2026-09-04) **[corrected]**; Brownnutt et al. 2015 Eqs. 14-18, whose printed Eq. 14 gives both Lindblad terms the decay structure and so has no absorption channel; the form below is the correct one, consistent with their Eqs. 18 and 24 **[corrected]**):

ρ̇ = −i(ω_t + δ)[a†a, ρ] + (Γ/2)(N̄ + 1)(2aρa† − a†aρ − ρa†a) + (Γ/2) N̄ (2a†ρa − aa†ρ − ρaa†),

with damping rate Γ, bath occupation N̄ and a noise-induced frequency shift δ. The short-time decay of the coherence of (|n₀⟩ + |m₀⟩)/√2 proceeds at (Γ/2)[(2N̄ + 1)(n₀ + m₀) + 2N̄] ≈ Γ_h(n₀ + m₀ + 1) for N̄ ≫ 1, the last term being the absorption channel acting on the lower level, so the scale is Γ_h and the enhancement over it is (n₀ + m₀ + 1), not Γ_h(2N̄ + 1)(n₀ + m₀) **[corrected; the Run 3 correction itself omitted the +1 and was caught by the 2026-09-04 critique and confirmed with `mesolve`, which gives 2.01, 4.03 and 10.09 Γ_h for (n₀, m₀) = (0, 1), (1, 2), (3, 6)]**; a quoted heating rate pins d⟨n⟩/dt at n = 0, that is ΓN̄, not the thermal diffusion rate Γ(2N̄ + 1). The heating rate from the ground state is Γ_h = Γ N̄, and because the effective bath temperature for electric-field noise is enormous (N̄ ≫ n̄ for any laboratory n̄), the rate equation ṅ = Γ(N̄ − n̄) gives a constant ṅ ≈ Γ_h independent of n̄ **[verified]** (this is RMP 2003's "high-temperature amplitude damping" **[verified]**). QuTiP collapse operators: √(Γ(N̄+1)) a and √(Γ N̄) a†, which in the high-temperature limit are both √Γ_h.

**Rate from field noise.** Γ_h = (e²/(4 m_I ħ ω_t)) S_E(ω_t) with S_E the single-sided spectral density, S_E(ω) = 2∫dτ ⟨δE(τ)δE(0)⟩ e^{−iωτ} **[verified]** (Brownnutt Eqs. 11-12). Turchette et al. 2000 write the same physics; the arXiv preprint (quant-ph/0002040) prints Eq. (3) as Γ = q² S_E/(m ħ ω), which does not follow from its Eq. (2), whereas the published Phys. Rev. A version prints q² S_E(ω_m)/(4 m ħ ω_m) with an explicitly single-sided S_E = 2∫dτ e^{iωτ}⟨ε(τ)ε(0)⟩ and is internally consistent (the errata check of 2026-09-04, Appendix D, re-derived its Eq. 3 from its Eq. 2 with zero residual and matched it to the first term of its Eq. 4), so the defect the research runs found is the preprint's and the version of record agrees with Brownnutt; with a two-sided density S_E^{(2)} = S_E^{(1)}/2 the same rate reads q² S_E^{(2)}/(2 m ħ ω) **[corrected: preprint only]**. The simulator stores S_E single-sided and converts on input; the same single-sided Γ_h = e²S_E/(4mħω) drives the splitting, transport and junction heating channels of Section 4.6, made time-dependent through ω(t) along a trajectory, and the equal-rate pair {√Γ a, √Γ a†} used by the transport sources is the N̄ ≫ 1 limit of the rows below rather than a competing convention **[corrected]**. With rf drive present the general form sums over micromotion sidebands, Γ_h = (e²/(4 m ħ ω_t)) Σ_j |C_{2j}|² S_E((b/2 + j) Ω_rf) **[verified]**, and Turchette's Eq. (4) gives the leading correction ṅ = (q²/(4mħω_m))[S_E(ω_m) + (ω_m²/(2Ω_T²)) S_E(Ω_T ± ω_m)], absent for axial motion **[verified]**; its weight ω_m²/(2Ω_T²) is Brownnutt's lowest-order Floquet weight |C_±2|² = q²/16 for the j = ±1 sidebands (they agree to 0.4% at q = 0.1), so the two must never be added together, and the published Turchette S_E definition is single-sided, consistent with the 1/4 in its Eq. 4; the two-sided reading that the first revision of this plan attributed to the paper is the preprint's (errata check of 2026-09-04) **[corrected: preprint only]**. Uncompensated stray fields add rf-voltage-noise heating at Ω_rf ± ω_t **[verified]** (Brownnutt Eq. 13, whose factor 16 decomposes as 4 × |C_eff|² with the equal in-phase gradient term doubling it).

**Multi-ion generalization.** Each mode heats at a rate driven by the mode-projected field-noise density Σ_{ij} b_i^{(k)} b_j^{(k)} S_E^{ij}(ω_k), where S_E^{ij} is the cross-spectral density between the fields at ions i and j (Brownnutt Eqs. 19-23) **[verified]**. Two limits are implemented as noise-model options: spatially uniform (perfectly correlated) noise heats only the centre-of-mass modes, at N times the single-ion rate, a statement that holds for equal masses only, because a mixed-species crystal has no mode orthogonal to the uniform displacement and every mode then heats at a rate ∝ (Σ_j c_j^{(k)}/√m_j)² (Section 4.1.7); uncorrelated noise heats every mode at the single-ion rate. The 9-ion measurement in Lechner et al. 2016, 65(7) quanta/s on the centre-of-mass mode against about 7.2 for a single ion in the same trap, is the one cross-apparatus-free check of the linear N scaling (9 × 7.2 = 65) **[verified]** and the King/Rohde disagreement about internal-mode heating (Section 12) are the empirical anchors; the correlation length is a device parameter. This matters more than it looks: every modern transverse two-qubit gate runs on a non-centre-of-mass mode whose eigenvector alternates in sign, exactly the case in which uniform-field noise cancels, so a centre-of-mass-only heating model predicts zero heating for the gate mode. The simulator never defaults to the uniform limit; it exposes the correlation length and, when a device supplies only one measured rate, attaches that rate to the gate mode with its provenance **[corrected]**.

**Empirical S_E.** The field parameterizes S_E ∝ ω^{−α} d^{−β} T^{γ} **[verified]** (Brownnutt Eq. 30 with its ranges; a quoted exponent may refer to S_E or to ṅ ∝ ω^{−(α+1)}, and α is not stable under surface treatment: 0(2) before and 6.0(2) after cleaning for a NIST molybdenum ring trap, 1.27(23) before and 0.95(28) after argon-ion cleaning for a copper-aluminium surface trap whose noise nevertheless fell a hundredfold) **[corrected]** with no a-priori justification; measured spans are Section 1's verified ranges (10⁻¹⁶ to 10⁻⁶ V² m⁻² Hz⁻¹, β from 2.6 to 4.0, α from 0.57 to 1.5), with Brownnutt's median values 40 × 10⁻¹² V² m⁻² Hz⁻¹ at 300 K and 0.2 × 10⁻¹² at 6 K for 30-230 µm traps **[verified]**. Johnson noise gives S_E = 4 k_B T R(ω)/d² **[verified]** and the fluctuating-patch model gives S_E = 3 C S_V r_p²/(4 a⁴), i.e. a d⁻⁴ scaling **[verified]**, with the 1/3 = ⟨cos²θ⟩ single-axis projection folded into the prefactor. The noise module ships these as named models with their literature parameters, and device presets carry documented values with conditions; no attempt is made to predict S_E from materials.

**Motional dephasing.** Trap-frequency fluctuations produce phase damping, whose reservoir Hamiltonian couples to a a† (RMP 2003 Eq. 163) **[verified]**. The simulator offers two representations: a Lindblad term √(γ_φ/2) a†a for fast (white) frequency noise, and quasi-static sampling of ω_k per shot for slow drift, the latter being the dominant laboratory effect for mode frequencies. The frequency shift δ of the master equation is folded into the calibrated mode frequency.

**Measuring n̄ and ṅ (calibration procedure).** For a thermal state the k-th red and blue sideband strengths after equal pulse durations obey I_k^{rsb} = [n̄/(1 + n̄)]^k I_k^{bsb}, a ratio independent of pulse time that inverts directly for n̄; the heating rate follows from linear regression of n̄ against an inserted delay (Turchette Eqs. 8-11) **[verified]**. This is the simulator's thermometry and heating-rate experiment, and Section 9 requires the extracted n̄ to match the state actually prepared.

#### 4.1.6 Surface-electrode traps from electrode geometry

No source of the first three runs treated surface-electrode geometry (Section 12), so the trap module accepted field curvatures only from an external field solution. This subsection adds the analytic layer that lets a user specify a planar trap by its electrode outlines and voltages. It rests on the gapless-plane approximation of House 2008 and Wesenberg 2008 (electrodes are coplanar, gaps between them have zero width, the plane outside the electrodes is grounded), whose transcription is being checked in Run 4 (Appendix D); the formulas below were derived and checked numerically on 2026-09-04 (`check_surface_mixed.py`) **[recomputed here]** and are tagged **[background]** until that check completes.

**Potential of one electrode.** In the half-space above a grounded plane, the potential produced by a patch held at unit voltage is the solid angle the patch subtends at the observation point divided by 2π. For a rectangle [x₁, x₂] × [y₁, y₂] in the plane z = 0 this is the closed form

φ(x, y, z) = (1/2π) Σ_{i,j=1,2} (−1)^{i+j} arctan[ (x_i − x)(y_j − y) / (z R_ij) ],  R_ij = [(x_i − x)² + (y_j − y)² + z²]^{1/2},

which satisfies Laplace's equation (finite-difference residual 10⁻⁸ at three test points), tends to 1 above the electrode and 0 elsewhere as z → 0, and reduces for an infinite strip (y₁ → −∞, y₂ → +∞) to φ = (1/π)[arctan((x₂ − x)/z) − arctan((x₁ − x)/z)] (agreement to 10⁻⁸). Any polygonal electrode is a signed sum of rectangles or is integrated numerically with the same kernel z/(2π|r − r′|³); the total potential is Σ_electrodes V_k φ_k with V_k the dc voltage or the rf amplitude, and the field is its analytic gradient. Finite gaps and finite electrode thickness are the corrections Wesenberg 2008 and Schmied 2010 treat; they are outside the first release and are the reason the module still accepts an external field solution.

**rf pseudopotential and the map to Mathieu parameters.** With the rf electrodes at amplitude V_rf and frequency Ω_rf, the ion of charge Q and mass m feels the pseudopotential Ψ(r) = Q²|E_rf(r)|²/(4mΩ_rf²) (the Dehmelt form already used in Section 4.1.1), and the total static potential is Ψ + Q Φ_dc. The rf null is the zero of E_rf; the principal axes and curvatures come from the Hessian of Ψ + QΦ_dc there; and the geometry-free map of Section 4.1.1, a_i = 4QΦ″_dc,i/(mΩ_rf²) and q_i = 2QΦ″_rf,i/(mΩ_rf²) with Φ″ the second derivatives along the principal axes, feeds the Mathieu module, so the secular frequencies, β and the micromotion amplitudes follow from the electrode drawing. The pseudopotential curvature reproduces the secular frequency only to lowest order in q (Section 4.1.1); the module reports both the pseudopotential and the Mathieu value.

**Two rf rails, closed forms** (House 2008 Eqs. 24-29; Nizamani and Hensinger 2012; Chiaverini et al. 2005) **[verified]**, **[corrected]**. With the rf electrodes infinitely long along the trap axis and occupying −c < x < 0 and a < x < a + b (a is the full width of the centre electrode between them; these are House's coordinates, and the null moves toward the narrower rail by a|b − c|/(2(b + c)), so Nizamani and Hensinger's x₀ = ac/(b + c), copied from House under a figure whose rail labels are House's mirror image, reads x₀ = ab/(b + c) in their own labelling whenever b ≠ c, a convention trap the module avoids by storing edges rather than widths), the rf field has an exact zero above the plane at

x₀ = ac/(b + c),  y₀ = [abc(a + b + c)]^{1/2}/(b + c),

so the symmetric five-wire trap (c = b) has its null at x₀ = a/2, y₀ = [2ab + a²]^{1/2}/2 and the four-wire limit (c = a, b → ∞) at y₀ = a; the five-wire height was derived independently from the strip potential and confirmed numerically (0.921954 for a = 1, b = 1.2) **[recomputed here]**. The rf curvature matrix at the null is traceless in the transverse plane, Q₁₁ = −Q₂₂, and its off-diagonal element Q₁₂ ∝ (b − c) vanishes for the symmetric trap, so the rf pseudopotential there is isotropic to second order (∂²|E|²/∂x² = ∂²|E|²/∂y² to 10⁻⁴ numerically **[recomputed here]**) and the transverse principal axes are set, and rotated, by the dc electrodes alone, exactly parallel and perpendicular to the substrate for a mirror-symmetric five-wire trap; an rf-width asymmetry w ± w/2 rotates them by about 30°, and the four-wire geometry gives about ±45°. That rotation is what lets one laser beam parallel to the surface cool both transverse modes. The trap depth of the symmetric trap with no dc contribution is the pseudopotential at the escape point x_E = a/2, y_E = [2ab + a² + 2(a + b)(2ab + a²)^{1/2}]^{1/2}/2,

ψ_E = (Z²e²V_rf²/(π²mΩ²)) [b/((a + b)² + (a + b)(2ab + a²)^{1/2})]²,

maximal at b/a ≈ 1.19149 for fixed a; Nizamani's form of the same depth, Ξ = e²V_rf²κ/(π²mΩ²y₀²) with κ = [2√(abc(a+b+c))/((2a+b+c)(2a+b+c+2√(a(a+b+c))))]², is algebraically identical to House's for c = b (checked **[recomputed here]**) and is maximal at fixed ion height for b/a = 3.676 (h = 1.445a; Nizamani prints 1.43a) with c = b, and at b/a = 4.902 (h = 1.362a; Nizamani prints 1.27a, a 7% error against his own closed form that would undersize a trap back-solved from a target height by 15% in depth and 33% in heating rate) for c = b/2 **[corrected]**; House's b/a = 1.19149 (fixed centre-electrode width) and Nizamani's 3.676 (fixed ion height) answer different questions and differ by 32% in depth at fixed height, so a design statement must say which is held fixed. In House's Mathieu notation the geometry enters through the matrices A_ij = (4Ze/(mΩ²)) ∂²φ_dc/∂x_i∂x_j and Q_ij = (2Ze/(mΩ²)) ∂²φ_rf/∂x_i∂x_j, both traceless by Laplace's equation, and when they do not commute the equations of motion do not separate, which is why the trap module's monodromy method (Section 4.1.1) integrates the coupled vector Mathieu equation rather than three scalar ones. Two sign facts about House's paper matter to an implementer: his Eq. 4 is ẍ + [A + 2Q cos 2τ]x = 0, so his Q is the negative of the q in the standard form ü + (a − 2q cos 2τ)u = 0 of Section 4.1.1 (magnitudes and stability unchanged, signs of off-diagonal terms not); and the lower-left block of his first-order matrix F(τ) in Eq. 7 is printed as +[A + 2Q cos 2τ] where the equations of motion require the minus sign, a misprint that his Hamiltonian-matrix check KF + FᵀK = 0 cannot detect and that reports a stable trap (a_z = 0.005, q = 0.20) as unstable with max|Re μ| = 0.0707 if copied **[corrected]**; U(0) in his Eq. 9 is the 6 × 6 identity. Nizamani and Hensinger's q = 2eV_rf/(mΩ²h²) is an rf stability factor normalized by the ion height h, not the trap's Mathieu q (their working point q ≈ 0.7 is in that normalization), and their Eq. 6 prints a 2e prefactor on the quartic axial expansion Ψ(z) = 2e(αz² + βz⁴) that is a factor 2 inconsistent with their Eq. 7 and with Home and Steane's convention, in which α and β are the coefficients of the electric potential V(z) ≈ V₀ − E₀z + αz² + βz⁴ and the potential energy is e(αz² + βz⁴) **[corrected]**. House's five-wire depth formula is derived for equal rail widths only, so Nizamani's c = b/2 branch of κ (maximal at b/a ≈ 4.90 with κ = 0.020038, exactly 8/9 of the c = b maximum 0.022542 at b/a ≈ 3.676) is an extrapolation whose exact gapless-plane saddle the module computes numerically rather than trusting the closed form **[corrected]**. The dc electrode voltages for a chosen axial curvature are the minimum-norm solution of House's Eq. 30: minimize Σ_i V_i² subject to Σ_i V_i ∇φ_i = 0 at the null (no stray field, hence no excess micromotion) and Σ_i V_i ∂²φ_i/∂z² = mΩ²A_zz/(4Ze), a linear system solved by Lagrange multipliers that needs at least four independent electrodes. Wesenberg 2008 supplies the general tools the module uses beyond rectangles: the field of any polygonal electrode as a Biot-Savart line integral around its boundary, the strip electrode's complex potential Φ(z) = (iV/π) Σ_i q_i ln(z − z_i), the result that a surface-electrode multipole of order n (a 2n-pole: n = 2 quadrupole, n = 3 hexapole, although Wesenberg's own Fig. 7c caption calls the n = 3 case an octupole) is strongest for an electrode angular width θ_w = π/n, where it reaches 2V/(π(2d)ⁿ) and is weaker than the best three-dimensional multipole at the same ion-electrode distance by 2ⁿπ/4, and the maximal intrinsic quadrupole depth (5√5 − 11)/(2π²) times U₀ = Q²V_rf²/(4MΩ²d²), 55.8 meV for his Eq. 11 parameters (Ω = 2π × 100 MHz, V_rf = 100 V, M = 10 u, d = 100 µm, where q₀ = 0.98 and the maximal secular frequency is 5.5 MHz), with the saddle at about n times the ion height for order n **[verified]**. Maximal depth and maximal strength coincide only for θ_w = π/n together with cos(nθ₀) = 0, which for the quadrupole is the antisymmetric four-wire geometry with rf edges at y/d = −1, 0, +1; the symmetric five-wire trap (θ₀ = π/2) reaches 0.9514 of the maximal depth, and Wesenberg's Sec. V A parenthetical (θ₀ = π/2, θ_w = π/2) for the maximum is an internal slip, as is the additive constant after his Eq. 21, which should read Vnθ_w/(2π) **[corrected]**. Chiaverini's line-charge closed forms for the four-wire trap are the analytic regression set: rf null at height √3/2 in units of the centre-to-centre spacing of adjacent rails (charges at ∓3/2 and ∓1/2), curvature 8/3, and a depth 1/72 and secular frequency 0.29 of the four-rod quadrupole under identical rf amplitude, mass, charge and axial potential; his numerical relaxation results (five-wire secular frequency 0.30 and depth 0.010 relative to a two-layer quadrupole at equal ion-electrode distance) are the regression numbers for the numerical path; and his principal-axis results fix the beam-geometry consequence of Section 4.2.1: the pseudopotential is cylindrically symmetric near the null, so the transverse axes are set by the dc potential, lie parallel and perpendicular to the substrate for the mirror-symmetric five-wire trap (a cooling beam parallel to the substrate then has no projection on the vertical mode), rotate by 30° when one rf rail is widened to 3w/2 and the other narrowed to w/2, and sit near ±45° for the four-wire geometry **[verified]**. Madsen et al. 2004's two-layer planar geometry adds the barrier location r_max = (a/2)(1 − 1/(πα)) along the weak transverse axis and the asymptotic scaled depth e²/(4mΩ_T²π²k_B) = 2589 K µm²/V² for 111Cd+ at Ω_T = 2π × 50 MHz, where the paper itself prints 2400 and 2694 inconsistently **[corrected]**.

**Conventions and fixtures Run 4 settled for surface traps** **[verified]**, **[corrected]**. Electrode widths are measured from gap centre to gap centre, which is what makes the gapless-plane parameters accurate to second rather than first order in the gap width (Nizamani's a = 60 µm is 50 µm of metal plus two 5 µm gaps); the ion height has one name, h, converted from House's y₀ and Wesenberg's d at ingest; V_rf is the peak of a cos(Ω_rf t) drive, which is what puts the 4 in Q²|E|²/(4mΩ²), so that a stored peak read as an rms amplitude (implied peak × √2) doubles every depth and inflates every rf-set secular frequency by √2, and one read as peak-to-peak (implied peak/2) quarters the depth and halves those frequencies, depth ∝ f² and frequency ∝ f always moving together (the second revision paired a halved depth with a √2-inflated frequency; derivation audit of 2026-09-04), and Chiaverini's and Madsen's V₀ are differential amplitudes with the pair at ±V₀/2; both rf rails are driven in phase at equal amplitude with everything else at rf ground; the unit-voltage basis functions are stored dimensionless, Θ = φ/V, with Σ_i Θ_i = 1 asserted on the electrode plane (every region, including the semi-infinite outer ones, is assigned a strip); House's four-arctan rectangle uses the principal branch of arctan (never atan2), needs its corners ordered x₁ < x₂ and z₁ < z₂ (one swap negates the potential), is odd in the height (it returns −φ below the plane), and equals the exact half-space Green's function to 10⁻¹⁵ with a discrete Laplacian of 1.8 × 10⁻⁷ at a step of 10⁻⁴. The ion sits at the minimum of the total potential, not at the rf null, once dc, stray fields and gravity act, and the residual rf field there is the excess micromotion of Section 4.1.1; the escape point is a saddle, found for the gapless plane from the algebraic condition G′(Z) = 0, a degree-six polynomial with one root in the upper half-plane, rather than by a search constrained to x = x₀ (0.5 to 1.3% high in depth for b ≠ c), and the b = c depth formula applied to unequal rails is 8.6 to 12.4% low. The maximal depth at fixed ion height is geometry independent: κ = 0.0225425 = (5√5 − 11)/8 is at once House's four-wire coefficient 1/(2(11 + 5√5)), Wesenberg's optimal-quadrupole bound and the numerically observed maximum for c/b from 1 to 0.1, one theorem derived three ways and agreeing to 10⁻¹⁶, so Nizamani's 8/9 for c = b/2 is a ratio of printed figures of merit and not a physical cost (the exact depth at his asymmetric design point is 0.12% below the symmetric optimum). The rf pseudopotential is exactly isotropic in the transverse plane at a two-dimensional rf null, because the field matrix is symmetric and traceless so that MᵀM = λ²𝟙, so the transverse principal axes are set by the dc potential alone and the angle of the rf Hessian is never a mode axis; asymmetric rails rotate the observed axes only by displacing the null, so that the dc Hessian is sampled elsewhere, and Chiaverini's 30° is one calculated example. Nizamani's q = 2eV_rf/(mΩ²h²) exceeds the local Mathieu q, built from the principal-axis curvature √(Q₁₁² + Q₁₂²) and not from |Q₁₁|, by a geometry-set factor, 3.2002 for his own trap (q_N = 0.6948 against q = 0.2171 at 500 V, and it is 0.2171 that reproduces his quoted 4.2 MHz), and his worked parameter set is not self-consistent (a = 60, b = 300, c = 150 µm give h = 82.46 µm, not 85; 450 V gives q_N = 0.589, not 0.7), so his closed forms transfer and his numbers are illustrative. **What this changes elsewhere.** Micromotion in a surface trap has an intrinsic component along the pseudopotential gradient wherever the ion is displaced from the rf null by stray fields (Section 4.1.1's excess micromotion), and the anomalous heating scale d in S_E ∝ d^{−β} (Section 4.1.5) is now the computed height h rather than a typed number; the module reports h and the trap depth with every device that uses it.

#### 4.1.7 Mixed-species crystals and the quantized time-dependent frame

The crystal module works from a per-ion mass list (Section 4.2.5) so that sympathetic cooling and the 9Be+-24Mg+ architecture of Home et al. 2009 fit without restructuring, and the first release exercises it only for single-species chains; Run 3 recorded that the mixed-species mode structure was supplied by no source in the set (Section 12). This subsection specifies it, and Run 4 checks the transcription against Kielpinski et al. 2000, Morigi and Walther 2001, Wübbena et al. 2012 and Home 2013 (Appendix D); the formulas are standard **[background]** and were checked numerically on 2026-09-04 **[recomputed here]**.

**Statics are mass-independent; dynamics are not.** The equilibrium positions of Section 4.1.2 depend only on the charges and the trap potential, so a mixed crystal with equal charges has the same equilibrium positions as a single-species chain in the same dc potential. The transverse confinement, however, comes from the rf pseudopotential, whose depth and curvature scale as 1/m, so that ω_r² ∝ 1/m² and ω_r ∝ q ∝ 1/m at fixed voltages (Section 4.1.1), so heavier ions are more weakly confined transversely while the axial dc confinement scales as 1/√m; a mixed crystal therefore has different single-ion frequencies per species and the small-oscillation problem is the generalized eigenproblem

K b = ω² M b,  K_ij = ∂²V/∂x_i∂x_j at equilibrium,  M = diag(m_1, …, m_N),

solved as the symmetric problem M^{−1/2} K M^{−1/2} c = ω² c with orthonormal eigenvectors c^{(k)}. The physical displacement of ion i in mode k is x_i = Σ_k c_i^{(k)} [ħ/(2 m_i ω_k)]^{1/2} (a_k + a_k†), so the Lamb-Dicke parameter of ion i on mode k for a drive of effective wavevector Δk is

η_{i,k} = c_i^{(k)} (Δk·ê_k) [ħ/(2 m_i ω_k)]^{1/2},

with the ion's own mass under the root and the mass-weighted eigenvector component in front; for equal masses c reduces to the b of Section 4.1.3 and this is the convention row "Lamb-Dicke base" of Section 13. This is Home 2013's Eq. 19, whose √(ħ/(2m_jω_α)) prefactor is correct only with orthonormal mass-weighted eigenvectors, which Run 4 verified together with the eigenproblem (Home Eqs. 7-9) and the quantization (Eqs. 10-13), with one misprint to override: Home's Eq. 13 writes the mass-weighted momentum as i(ħ/(√2σ′_α))(a_α − a_α†), which gives [ζ̂′, p̂′] = −iħ against his own Eq. 12; the sign-corrected (a_α† − a_α) is implemented **[verified]**, **[corrected]**. Home's other printed slips, each confirmed by the run: Eq. 4 carries a cos(Ω_rf t) that his Eq. 1 already supplies (the drive counted twice); Eq. 14 drops the 1/√2 of Eq. 12, so his σ′_α = √(ħ/ω_α) is √2 times the rms zero-point amplitude; Eq. 21 writes the interaction-picture phases in the complex-conjugate convention, so mixing it with a standard frame swaps the red and blue sidebands; Eq. 24's carrier prefactor is Ω, not Ω/2, and its validity condition is η²(2n + 1) ≪ 1; Eq. 27 prints √m_i inside a sum over j; Eq. 28 indexes η by the wrong ion; Eq. 29's second term is an energy that needs division by ħω_α; his "50× weaker" 24Mg+ sideband coupling is 91× on the 12.11 MHz x mode once √(m_Mg/m_Be) is included, 8300× in rate; and his Table I caption transposes the Mg x and y frequencies (below) **[corrected]**. Every eigenvector in every source (Kielpinski's v, Morigi and Walther's β′, Wübbena's b, Home's e′, the columns of Landa's U) is an eigenvector of the mass-weighted Hessian, orthonormal in u_i = √m_i z_i and never in raw displacements, the one exception being Morigi and Walther's oblique Eq. 9 (convert with β′_i = √(m_i/m) q_i); renormalizing in raw displacements is a 20 to 160% error that vanishes identically at μ = 1, so no equal-mass test catches it (b₁² = 0.40641 instead of 0.31608 at μ = 40/27, the module's negative control). Mode order is not stable in μ (Kielpinski's three-ion spectrum crosses exactly at μ = 17/3) and "in-phase is lowest" holds axially but not radially, where the in-phase mode is the upper one (Wübbena Eqs. 15-16: ω_i² − ω_o² = a/μ² > 0, with the discriminant identity a² = Y² + μ³), so the module sorts by value and identifies modes by eigenvector structure; the overall sign of an eigenvector is a gauge fixed by the rule of Section 13 and the relative signs within a mode are physical, so published η tables (Morigi and Walther's are −1 times their printed Eq. 9; Home's Table II uses the opposite choice of which ion sits at +z) are compared up to a per-mode sign and a global relabelling. Equilibria are mass independent only for equal charges, and the solver must handle multiple minima and off-axis equilibria under a stray field (Home's Table II case sits 399 and 1583 nm off axis, and an on-axis start converges to a saddle), so it minimizes from a perturbed start and checks the Hessian's positivity. Kielpinski's and Wübbena's transverse closed forms assume equal dc curvatures in x and y (their α_dc = ½), an explicit parameter in the module because every transverse anchor moves with it (Kielpinski's 27.6 and 14.7 MHz become 30.2 and 16.3 at α_dc = 1), and ω_r0 there is the bare pseudopotential frequency, the observed ω_x = ω_z√(ε² − ½) being 26.7 MHz for ω_r0 = 27.6 MHz. **The quantized multi-ion time-dependent frame** (Landa, Drewsen, Reznik, Retzker, J. Phys. A 45, 455305 (2012); Kaufmann et al. 2012) **[verified]**, **[corrected]**. The coupled Mathieu system ü + [A − 2Q cos 2t]u = 0 (A and Q the matrices of Section 4.1.6 in dimensionless time) is solved for each stable mode by a Fourier-Floquet series u = Σ_n C_{2n}[b e^{i(2n+β)t} + c e^{−i(2n+β)t}], whose coefficient vectors obey the three-term matrix recursion Q C_{2n−2} = [A − (2n + β)²] C_{2n} − Q C_{2n+2}, folded into two infinite continued matrix inversions whose consistency condition fixes β (the multi-mode generalization of Section 4.1.1's scalar recursion); the coefficient vectors then follow C₀ by continued inversions in both directions, C_{2n} = T_{2n}QC_{2n−2} upward and C_{−2n} = T̃_{−2n}QC_{−2n+2} downward with T̃_{−2n} = [R_{−2n} − QT̃_{−2n−2}Q]⁻¹, where the paper's text after its Eq. 25 writes the first downward step with an undefined inverted operator, a typo an implementer must not copy **[corrected]**. The π-periodic matrices U(t) (columns Σ_n C_{2n,β_j} e^{2int}) and V(t) (the same with factors i(2n + β_j)) form the Floquet-Lyapunov transformation φ = Γ(t)χ, which is canonical only under the normalization Vᵗ(0)U(0) = i/2 (the identity the Run 3 verifier flagged); with it the inverse of Γ is given by transposes and conjugates, the transformed Hamiltonian is a sum of decoupled constant-frequency oscillators Σ_j β_j ξ_j ζ_j plus a c-number ordering term, and the quantized ladder operators evolve freely, ζ̂_j(t) = ζ̂_j(0)e^{−iβ_j t}, with exact oscillator commutators; the Floquet number states are closed-form multidimensional Hermite functions of U and V, and the single-ion reduction reproduces Leibfried et al. 2003's Eq. 36 (the RMP, which Landa's citation lists as a Wineland review) with t → Ωt/2 and ν → νΩ/2, the RMP's printed form itself carrying a sign misprint in its x′² coefficient. U(t) is diagonal, and the frame reduces to constant normal modes with factorized wavefunctions, only when a constant matrix diagonalizes the equations of motion, for instance when A and Q commute; otherwise the modes are irreducibly time dependent, and Γ⁻¹(0) is not unitary in general (under Landa's own normalization it is non-unitary for every admissible β ≠ 1, including the decoupled case, so unitarity is never a sanity check; the closed form Γ⁻¹ = [[iV†, −iU†], [−iVᵗ, iUᵗ]] is) **[verified]**.
